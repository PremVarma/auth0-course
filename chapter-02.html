Chapter 2 — History of Authentication

Every authentication technology you will ever configure in Auth0 — passwords, MFA, social login, SAML, OIDC, passkeys — exists because something before it broke. This chapter walks the 60-year arc from a plaintext password file on a 1960s mainframe to passwordless WebAuthn, and at every step asks the only question that matters: what attack or pain forced the next invention? Understand the chain of failures and every Auth0 feature stops being magic and starts being inevitable.

1. Theory — First Principles of "Who Are You?"

Before we touch a single protocol, fix three words that beginners constantly confuse. Every system in this chapter is built out of these three primitives, so if you blur them you will misread every diagram that follows.

TermQuestion it answersPlain example
IdentificationWho do you claim to be?Typing alice@acme.com into a login box.
Authentication (AuthN)Can you prove that claim?Entering the password only Alice should know.
Authorization (AuthZ)What are you allowed to do?Alice can read billing, but cannot delete users.

This whole chapter is about the middle column — proof of identity — and how the kind of proof we accept has evolved. Proof always comes from one or more of three authentication factors:

  • Something you know — a password, PIN, or the answer to "your first pet's name". Cheap, but copyable: if I learn it, I am you.
  • Something you have — a phone, a hardware key, a smartcard. Harder to copy, but can be lost or stolen.
  • Something you are — a fingerprint, face, or iris. Convenient, but cannot be changed once leaked.

The entire history of authentication is one long migration: from relying on a single knowledge factor that turned out to be trivially stealable, toward combining possession and inherence factors that an attacker on the other side of the planet cannot reproduce. A second, parallel story runs alongside it — delegation: how do I prove who I am to many systems without handing my password to each one? That second thread gives us SSO, SAML, OAuth, and OpenID Connect.

Key

Two engines drive this entire history: (1) making the proof harder to steal (passwords → MFA → passkeys), and (2) making the proof reusable across systems without sharing the secret (sessions → SSO → SAML → OAuth → OIDC). Keep both engines in mind and the timeline organizes itself.

2. Story — Sixty Years in the Life of a Login

Meet CTSS, the Compatible Time-Sharing System at MIT, around 1961. For the first time, many people share one expensive computer, each with private files. The designers need a way to keep Bob out of Alice's files, so they invent the password: a secret word stored next to each username in a file called UACCNT.SECRET. It works — until 1962, when a researcher named Allan Scherr wants more machine time than his allocation allows. He simply prints the password file. The world's first password database leak happens roughly a year after the world's first password. This is the recurring pattern of the entire chapter: invent a defense, the attacker adapts, invent the next defense.

Fast-forward through the decades like a film reel. 1976: Robert Morris and Ken Thompson, building Unix, realize storing passwords in plaintext is insane, so they hash them and sprinkle in salt. 1980s: companies buy hundreds of computers and printers — managing a user account on each one is a nightmare, so directory services (X.500, then LDAP) centralize "who exists and where". 1993: MIT releases Kerberos so a user logs in once and gets tickets for every campus service. 1994: Netscape invents the HTTP cookie, giving the stateless web a memory and making web logins possible. 2002–2005: the enterprise web needs cross-company single sign-on; SAML is born. 2007–2012: "Sign in with Twitter/Facebook/Google" demands delegated access without password sharing — OAuth 1.0 → 2.0. 2014: OAuth was never about identity, so OpenID Connect bolts a real login layer on top, standardizing the JWT ID token. 2010s: phishing and breaches make passwords-alone indefensible — MFA and then passwordless go mainstream. 2019–2022: the FIDO Alliance and W3C ship WebAuthn / FIDO2, and Apple, Google, and Microsoft launch passkeys — finally, a credential that cannot be phished because it never leaves your device.

Auth0, the platform this course is about, sits at the end of this story: it is a single service that speaks all of these protocols so you don't have to re-implement six decades of hard-won security yourself.

4. Real-World Analogy — The Hotel and the Festival

Passwords are like a house key you carry: prove possession, get in. But a copied key is as good as the original, and if a burglar photographs it, they can cut a duplicate — exactly like a phished password.

Sessions/cookies are a hotel wristband. You show ID once at the front desk (login), they give you a wristband (session cookie), and for the rest of your stay the pool, gym, and bar just check the band instead of re-verifying your passport on every visit. Lose the wristband and whoever finds it can use the pool — which is why session hijacking is dangerous and why bands expire at checkout (session timeout).

Kerberos is a theme park. At the gate you trade your ticket for a wristband (a Ticket-Granting Ticket). Each ride doesn't re-check your purchase; it checks a per-ride stamp the central booth issues from your wristband. The rides never see your credit card (your password) — only short-lived stamps.

SAML / OIDC SSO is the airport. Your government issued a passport (the Identity Provider). Airlines, duty-free, and lounges (the apps) don't run their own background checks — they trust the passport. Show it once and a boarding pass gets you through many gates. OAuth is valet parking: you hand over a valet key that starts the car and opens the doors but won't open the trunk or glovebox — limited, scoped, revocable access, without giving away your master key (your password).

Passkeys are a tamper-proof, biometric-locked ID that, by law, can never be photocopied and only works at the genuine counter — wave a fake counter at it and it refuses. That "refuses the fake counter" property is what makes passkeys un-phishable.

5. Internal Working — How Each Mechanism Actually Functions

5.1 Plaintext & hashed passwords

A plaintext store keeps alice : hunter2 literally on disk. Login = string compare. The fatal flaw: anyone who reads the file (a leak, a backup, a rogue admin) instantly knows everyone's password — and people reuse passwords, so one leak cascades everywhere.

A hash is a one-way function: easy to compute H(password), computationally infeasible to invert. We store the hash, never the password. At login we hash the submitted value and compare hashes. But two more attacks remain: (1) identical passwords produce identical hashes, letting attackers spot reuse and precompute rainbow tables; the fix is a salt — a unique random value mixed in per user so H(salt + password) differs even for the same password. (2) Fast hashes (MD5, SHA-256) let attackers try billions of guesses per second; the fix is a slow, memory-hard hash (bcrypt, scrypt, Argon2) with a tunable cost factor, deliberately making each guess expensive.

 REGISTER:  password --+--> [ Argon2(salt, cost) ] --> store {salt, hash}
                        |
 LOGIN:     attempt ----+--> [ Argon2(stored salt, cost) ] --> compare == stored hash?
                                                                 |
                                                       constant-time compare (no timing leak)

5.2 LDAP & directory services / Active Directory

A directory is a hierarchical, read-optimized database of "things in an org": users, groups, computers, printers. LDAP (Lightweight Directory Access Protocol, 1993) is the network protocol to query it. Entries are named by a Distinguished Name (DN) like cn=alice,ou=eng,dc=acme,dc=com — read right-to-left like a path. To authenticate, a client performs an LDAP bind: it sends the DN and password; the server verifies them against the stored credential. Active Directory (AD) is Microsoft's directory product — it speaks LDAP and Kerberos and DNS, and is the backbone of nearly every corporate Windows network. Auth0 connects to AD/LDAP via the AD/LDAP Connector so enterprise users keep their existing corporate credentials.

5.3 Kerberos — tickets and the KDC

Kerberos solves "log in once, access many services, and never send the password over the wire after that". The trusted third party is the Key Distribution Center (KDC), with two halves: the Authentication Server (AS) and the Ticket-Granting Server (TGS). Everything rests on symmetric encryption and the assumption that the KDC shares a secret key with every principal.

  CLIENT                    KDC                          SERVICE (e.g. file server)
    |   1. AS-REQ (I am alice) |                                   |
    |------------------------->|  AS checks alice exists           |
    |   2. AS-REP: TGT +       |  encrypts TGT with KDC key,       |
    |      session key (enc.)  |  session key with alice's key     |
    |<-------------------------|                                   |
    |   3. TGS-REQ: TGT + "I   |                                   |
    |      want fileserver"    |                                   |
    |------------------------->|  TGS validates TGT                |
    |   4. TGS-REP: SERVICE    |                                   |
    |      TICKET (enc. w/ svc |                                   |
    |      key) + new sess key |                                   |
    |<-------------------------|                                   |
    |   5. AP-REQ: service ticket + authenticator                  |
    |------------------------------------------------------------->|
    |   6. (optional) mutual auth reply                            |
    |<-------------------------------------------------------------|
   The password is used ONCE (step 2 decryption) and never transmitted.
   Tickets are time-stamped & short-lived => stolen ticket expires fast.

5.4 Cookies & server-side sessions

HTTP is stateless: each request is independent, so the server forgets you instantly. To stay "logged in", on successful login the server creates a session (a server-side record keyed by a random session ID) and sends that ID to the browser in a Set-Cookie header. The browser returns the cookie on every subsequent request; the server looks up the session and knows it's you. The cookie holds only an opaque random ID — not your identity data — so stealing it grants access only until it expires or is revoked server-side.

5.5 SAML & web SSO

SAML (Security Assertion Markup Language, 2.0 in 2005) is XML-based browser SSO between organizations. Three roles: the Service Provider (SP) = the app, the Identity Provider (IdP) = where you log in, and the user in a browser. The IdP issues a signed XML assertion ("this is alice, here are her attributes"), and the SP trusts it because it verifies the IdP's signature against a pre-exchanged certificate.

  Browser            SP (app)                 IdP (Auth0 / Okta / ADFS)
    | GET /app          |                         |
    |------------------>| 302 + SAML AuthnRequest |
    |<------------------|                         |
    | redirect w/ request ---------------------->|  user logs in here
    |                                            |  IdP builds SIGNED assertion
    |<------ 302 back to SP w/ SAMLResponse ------|
    | POST SAMLResponse |                         |
    |------------------>| verify signature, read  |
    |                   | assertion -> set session|
    |<-- app content ---|                         |

5.6 OAuth 1.0 → 2.0

OAuth solves delegated authorization: let app X act on your behalf at service Y without giving X your Y password. OAuth 1.0 (community spec 2007; formalized as RFC 5849 in 2010) worked but required every request to be cryptographically signed by the client — painful and error-prone. OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749, 2012) dropped per-request signing in favor of TLS for transport security and bearer tokens, and introduced multiple grant types (flows) for different app types. The dominant flow today is Authorization Code + PKCE.

  USER     CLIENT APP        AUTH SERVER          RESOURCE API
   |  click "connect" |           |                    |
   |----------------->| redirect to /authorize         |
   |                  |---------->| user consents       |
   |                  |<----------| 302 ?code=abc       |
   |                  | POST /token (code + PKCE verif) |
   |                  |---------->|                     |
   |                  |<----------| access_token        |
   |                  | GET /data (Bearer access_token) |
   |                  |------------------------------->|
   |                  |<-------------------------------|  data

5.7 OpenID Connect & JWT

OAuth 2.0 grants access but deliberately says nothing standardized about who the user is. OpenID Connect (OIDC, 2014) is a thin identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0. It adds: an ID Token (a JWT describing the authenticated user), a standard /userinfo endpoint, and a discovery document at /.well-known/openid-configuration. A JWT (JSON Web Token) is three Base64URL parts — header.payload.signature — where the payload carries claims (sub, iss, aud, exp) and the signature lets any party verify the token wasn't tampered with, without calling back to the issuer.

   eyJhbGciOiJSUzI1NiJ9 . eyJzdWIiOiJhbGljZSIsImV4cCI6.. . SIGNATURE
   |__ HEADER (alg,typ) _|  |____ PAYLOAD (claims) _______|  |_ verifies _|
        which algorithm          who (sub), issuer (iss),       integrity
                                 audience (aud), expiry (exp)

5.8 MFA / 2FA & passwordless

MFA requires proof from two or more different factor categories. Common second factors: TOTP (a 6-digit code derived from a shared secret + the current 30-second time window, per RFC 6238), push approval, and SMS OTP (weakest — vulnerable to SIM swap). Passwordless removes the knowledge factor entirely: a magic link emails you a one-time URL, or an OTP texts/emails a one-time code — the channel itself proves possession.

5.9 Passkeys, FIDO2, WebAuthn

This is the endgame. WebAuthn (the browser/W3C API) plus CTAP (device protocol) together form FIDO2. On registration, your device generates a public/private key pair scoped to that exact site's domain. The private key never leaves the device; only the public key is registered with the server. To log in, the server sends a random challenge; the device signs it with the private key after a local biometric/PIN check; the server verifies the signature with the stored public key. Because the key is bound to the real origin, a phishing site (wrong domain) cannot trigger a valid signature — that origin binding is why passkeys are un-phishable. A passkey is simply a FIDO2 credential that syncs across your devices (via iCloud Keychain, Google Password Manager, etc.).

  REGISTER:  device generates {private (stays), public (sent)} for origin=acme.com
             server stores public key for alice

  LOGIN:     server --> random challenge -->
             device: verify origin == acme.com? user biometric OK?
                     sign(challenge) with private key
             <-- signed challenge --
             server verifies signature with stored public key  => logged in
             (fake-phish.com challenge is REFUSED: origin mismatch)

6. Code Walkthrough — Watch Each Era in Real Code

6.1 The bad old days: plaintext (NEVER ship this)

// ANTI-PATTERN — shown only so you can recognize and reject it.
if (submittedPassword.equals(user.getPlainPassword())) {   // stored in cleartext
    grantAccess();   // one DB leak compromises every user, everywhere they reuse it
}

6.2 The fix: salted, slow hashing (Spring Boot, BCrypt/Argon2)

import org.springframework.security.crypto.argon2.Argon2PasswordEncoder;

// cost-tuned, salt is generated & embedded automatically inside the hash string
PasswordEncoder enc = Argon2PasswordEncoder.defaultsForSpringSecurity_v5_8();

String stored = enc.encode("hunter2");
// $argon2id$v=19$m=16384,t=2,p=1$<salt>$<hash>  -- salt & params travel WITH the hash

boolean ok = enc.matches("hunter2", stored);  // constant-time compare under the hood

6.3 Cookies & server-side sessions (Node.js + Express)

import express from "express";
import session from "express-session";

const app = express();
app.use(session({
  secret: process.env.SESSION_SECRET,   // signs the cookie so it can't be forged
  resave: false, saveUninitialized: false,
  cookie: { httpOnly: true, secure: true, sameSite: "lax", maxAge: 3600_000 }
  //        ^JS can't read   ^HTTPS only  ^CSRF defense   ^1h expiry
}));

app.post("/login", (req, res) => {
  // ...verify password against the Argon2 hash above...
  req.session.userId = user.id;          // server-side state; cookie holds only the ID
  res.send("logged in");
});

app.get("/me", (req, res) => {
  if (!req.session.userId) return res.status(401).send("nope");
  res.json({ userId: req.session.userId });
});

6.4 Decoding & verifying a JWT / OIDC ID token (FastAPI + python-jose)

from fastapi import FastAPI, Header, HTTPException
from jose import jwt
import httpx

app = FastAPI()
ISSUER   = "https://YOUR_TENANT.us.auth0.com/"
AUDIENCE = "https://api.myapp.com"
JWKS     = httpx.get(f"{ISSUER}.well-known/jwks.json").json()  # public signing keys

@app.get("/secure")
def secure(authorization: str = Header(...)):
    token = authorization.removeprefix("Bearer ").strip()
    try:
        claims = jwt.decode(
            token, JWKS, algorithms=["RS256"],
            audience=AUDIENCE, issuer=ISSUER,   # verifies signature + aud + iss + exp
        )
    except Exception:
        raise HTTPException(401, "invalid token")
    return {"hello": claims["sub"]}            # sub = the verified user id

The architectural principle distilled from 60 years of history: centralize the proof, distribute the trust. One hardened identity provider verifies users (so you don't reinvent password hashing, MFA, and SAML six times); every downstream service merely verifies a signed token using public keys it already trusts. That is precisely the SSO model the timeline kept reaching for.

10. Performance Considerations

  • Password hashing is intentionally slow. Argon2/bcrypt cost factors must be tuned so login takes ~100–300 ms — fast enough for users, painful for crackers. Too high a cost lets attackers DoS your login endpoint; rate-limit it.
  • Stateful sessions vs. stateless JWTs. Server sessions require a lookup (and shared store like Redis when you have many servers), but are instantly revocable. JWTs need no lookup — verify the signature locally and scale horizontally — but are valid until they expire, so revocation is harder. This trade-off (revocability vs. scalability) is a classic system-design interview topic.
  • JWKS caching. Verify JWT signatures with cached public keys (JWKS); don't fetch keys per request. Cache them and refresh on key rotation (watch the kid header).
  • Token size. JWTs travel in every request header — bloated custom claims add latency at scale. Keep payloads lean; fetch heavy profile data from /userinfo when needed.
  • Passkey UX is fast. One biometric tap, no typing, no SMS round-trip — passkeys are typically the fastest login of all while being the most secure.

11. Common Mistakes

Warn

Rolling your own crypto/auth. The single most expensive mistake. Six decades of subtle failures live in this chapter; use vetted libraries and providers like Auth0.

  • Storing passwords with fast hashes (MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256) or — unforgivably — plaintext.
  • Forgetting per-user salts, enabling rainbow-table attacks.
  • Confusing authentication (who you are) with authorization (what you can do) — and using an OAuth access token as proof of identity (use the OIDC ID token for that).
  • Not verifying the JWT signature (or accepting alg: none) — decoding is not verifying. Always validate signature, iss, aud, and exp.
  • Cookies missing HttpOnly/Secure/SameSite, exposing sessions to XSS and CSRF.
  • Relying on SMS OTP as a strong second factor (SIM swap).
  • Treating any single factor as "secure enough" against phishing.
  • Putting a client secret in a browser/SPA — use PKCE, never embed secrets in public clients.

12. Best Practices

  • Delegate identity to a dedicated provider (Auth0) instead of hand-rolling it.
  • Hash with Argon2id (or bcrypt), unique salt per user, tuned cost, constant-time compare.
  • Use OIDC Authorization Code + PKCE for user-facing apps; Client Credentials for machine-to-machine.
  • Always validate JWTs fully: signature (via JWKS), iss, aud, exp, and required scopes.
  • Set cookies HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite=Lax; keep sessions short and rotate on privilege change.
  • Enforce MFA; prefer passkeys/WebAuthn and push-with-number-matching over SMS.
  • Use short-lived access tokens with rotating refresh tokens; bind tokens to an audience.
  • Move consumers toward passwordless/passkeys to eliminate phishable secrets entirely.

13. Interview Questions (with Model Answers)

Q1. Why did we move from plaintext to salted, slow hashes?

Model answer: Plaintext means a single DB leak exposes every credential, and reuse spreads the damage to other sites. Hashing makes the stored value non-reversible. Salting defeats rainbow tables and hides reuse by making identical passwords hash differently. A slow, memory-hard function (Argon2/bcrypt) makes each brute-force guess expensive, so even leaked hashes resist offline cracking.

Q2. Explain the difference between authentication and authorization, and which token carries which.

Model answer: Authentication proves who you are; authorization decides what you may do. In OIDC, the ID token (a JWT) answers authentication — it describes the user. The access token (OAuth) answers authorization — it grants scoped access to an API. Using an access token to identify a user is a classic mistake.

Q3. How does Kerberos avoid sending the password over the network repeatedly?

Model answer: The password is only used locally to decrypt the AS reply once. After that the client holds a Ticket-Granting Ticket and session keys; for each service it obtains a short-lived service ticket from the TGS. Tickets are time-stamped and encrypted with keys the client never sees, so the password itself never traverses the wire after initial login, and stolen tickets expire quickly.

Q4. Why was OAuth 2.0 created, and how does it differ from OAuth 1.0?

Model answer: OAuth solves delegated access without sharing passwords. OAuth 1.0 required cryptographically signing every request — secure but hard to implement correctly. OAuth 2.0 relies on TLS for transport security and uses bearer tokens plus distinct grant types for different client types, dramatically simplifying adoption. The trade-off is that bearer tokens must be protected in transit and given short lifetimes.

Q5. Stateful sessions vs. stateless JWTs — when do you choose each?

Model answer: Sessions are server-side, easy to revoke instantly, but need a shared store to scale and a lookup per request. JWTs are self-contained — verify locally, scale horizontally with no central lookup — but are valid until expiry, making revocation harder (mitigated with short TTLs, refresh-token rotation, and denylists). Choose sessions when instant revocation matters most; choose JWTs for stateless, distributed APIs.

Q6. Why are passkeys considered un-phishable when passwords and even MFA are not?

Model answer: A passkey is a private key bound to a specific origin (domain) and stored on the device; it never transmits a reusable secret. Authentication is a challenge-response signed locally after a biometric/PIN check. A phishing site has a different origin, so the authenticator refuses to produce a valid signature. There is no code to read aloud, no secret to type into a fake page — removing the human-in-the-loop weakness that phishing exploits.

Q7. What must a backend validate before trusting a JWT?

Model answer: The signature (using the issuer's public key from JWKS, with the expected algorithm — never none), the issuer (iss), the audience (aud) so a token for another API is rejected, the expiry (exp) and not-before (nbf), and any required scopes/permissions for the operation.

14. Certification Tips

Tip

The Auth0/Okta certification exams love precise vocabulary. Lock these in:

  • IdP vs SP (SAML), Authorization Server vs Resource Server (OAuth) — know who plays which role.
  • OAuth defines authorization; OIDC adds the identity layer (ID token, /userinfo, discovery).
  • Remember the grant types: Authorization Code + PKCE (web/SPA/native), Client Credentials (M2M). Implicit and ROPC are legacy/discouraged.
  • ID token = identity; access token = API access; refresh token = get new tokens silently.
  • Auth0 Connections map to history: Database (passwords), Social (OAuth), Enterprise (SAML/OIDC/AD-LDAP), Passwordless, Passkeys.
  • Know standard JWT claims (iss, sub, aud, exp, iat, nbf, jti) and that RS256 (asymmetric) is preferred over HS256 for distributed verification.

20. Key Takeaways

Key

Authentication history is a chain of break → fix → new break. Each technology exists because the previous one failed against a specific attack — usually some form of secret theft or phishing.

Key

Two engines drive everything: making proof harder to steal (passwords → MFA → passkeys) and making proof reusable across systems without sharing the secret (sessions → SSO → SAML → OAuth → OIDC).

Key

AuthN ≠ AuthZ. OIDC ID tokens establish identity; OAuth access tokens grant scoped access. Never confuse the two, and always verify a JWT — decoding is not verifying.

Key

Passkeys are the destination. By binding a non-extractable private key to the real origin, they remove the phishable secret that every prior method depended on.

Key

Auth0 is the modern embodiment of this whole timeline: one provider that correctly implements password hashing, SSO, SAML, OAuth, OIDC, MFA, passwordless, and passkeys — so you configure protocols instead of reinventing 60 years of security.
