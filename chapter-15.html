Phase 7 · Enterprise Identity

Chapter 15 — Enterprise Identity: SSO, SLO and Multi-Tenant SaaS

Up to now you have authenticated individual users. Enterprise identity is a different game: you authenticate users who belong to companies, each of which wants its own login experience, its own corporate identity provider, its own admins, and its own walls. This chapter takes you from the raw mechanics of Single Sign-On (three independent session layers you must reason about), through the genuinely hard problem of Single Logout, and into Auth0 Organizations — the feature that turns a multi-tenant SaaS dream into a shippable architecture. By the end you will understand SSO and SLO at the protocol level, design a B2B identity model from first principles, wire connections per organization with home-realm discovery, onboard members by invitation, and defend every decision in an interview.

1. Theory — First Principles of Enterprise Identity

Start with the word tenant. In SaaS, a tenant is an isolated customer — usually a company. "Multi-tenant" means one running application instance serves many companies at once, keeping their data and configuration logically separated. The moment you sell to companies (B2B) rather than to people (B2C), three new requirements appear that B2C never had:

  1. Company-scoped identity. A user is not just alice@example.com; she is Alice, a member of Acme Corp. The same human might also be a member of Globex through a consulting gig. Identity is now a relationship: (user, organization).
  2. Bring-your-own IdP. Acme already runs Okta or Microsoft Entra ID (Azure AD). Acme's CISO will refuse to let employees create yet another password in your app. They demand that login federates to their identity provider so they keep control of provisioning, MFA, and offboarding.
  3. Delegated administration. Acme wants to invite and remove its own people without filing a support ticket with you. Administration must be partitioned per tenant.

Single Sign-On (SSO) is the property that once a user authenticates once, they can access multiple applications without re-entering credentials. The first principle behind SSO is that authentication state lives in a session, and a session is just a credential the server issues and the client presents on later requests (typically a cookie holding a session id or a signed token). SSO works by having multiple apps trust a shared authoritative session held at a central authority — in our world, the Auth0 tenant.

Single Logout (SLO) is the inverse and far harder property: a single logout action should terminate the user's session everywhere. It is hard precisely because SSO created multiple independent sessions across different security domains, and there is no reliable, synchronous channel to kill all of them at once.

Key

SSO is "many apps, one login because they share one session." Enterprise SSO adds "and that one session may itself be federated to the customer's own IdP." Every hard problem in this chapter — SLO, multi-tenancy, home-realm discovery — is a direct consequence of there being more than one session layer.

2. Story-Based Explanation

You built ProjectPilot, a project-management SaaS. The first 500 customers were freelancers who signed up with email + password. Life was simple.

Then Acme Corp wants 2,000 seats — but with conditions. Their IT writes: "All employees must log in with their Acme Okta accounts. When we fire someone in Okta, they must lose ProjectPilot access immediately. When an employee clicks 'Log out', they should be logged out of ProjectPilot and our internal portal." A week later Globex signs — same demands, but they use Microsoft Entra ID, and they want their own branded login page at globex.projectpilot.com.

Your old "one connection, one user pool" model collapses. Alice the consultant has accounts at both Acme and Globex and needs to switch between them. Bob is an Acme employee who must never be able to use a password — only Acme's Okta. The Acme admin wants to invite 50 new hires by email without involving you. And both companies expect that when their employees log out, the session everywhere dies.

This is exactly what Auth0 Organizations plus a deliberate session and logout strategy solves. The rest of the chapter is the story of building ProjectPilot's enterprise tier correctly.

4. Real-World Analogy

Think of a large conference venue (Auth0) hosting many company booths (organizations), inside which are several meeting rooms (your applications).

  • SSO = the wristband. At the entrance you show your conference badge once and get a wristband (the Auth0 SSO cookie). Every room you enter just checks the wristband — no re-badging. That is SSO: one authentication, many doors.
  • The corporate IdP = your employer's HR desk. Acme employees don't get their badge from the venue; they bring an Acme credential, and the venue's entrance phones Acme's HR desk to confirm "yes, this person works here" (federation). The venue never stores their password.
  • SLO = leaving the building. Cutting your wristband at the exit is easy. But you may also have left your coat in three rooms (app sessions) and your employer's HR desk still thinks you're "checked in." True SLO means someone has to walk to every room and phone HR to mark you out — which is exactly why it's unreliable.
  • Organizations = the booths. The same consultant (Alice) can have a pass for both the Acme booth and the Globex booth, and which booth she's standing in determines what she can see.

5. Internal Working — Cookies, Tokens and the prompt Parameter

5.1 How SSO reuse actually happens

When your app sends the browser to https://YOUR_TENANT.auth0.com/authorize, Auth0 looks for its SSO cookie on the auth0.com domain. This cookie was set on a previous login and represents the Auth0 session (Layer 2). If it exists and is valid, Auth0 can skip the login screen and immediately return a new authorization code to the requesting app — that is silent SSO. The decisive control is the OIDC prompt parameter:

prompt valueBehaviourUse it for
(omitted)Reuse Auth0 session if present, else show loginNormal login
noneNever show UI; succeed silently or error login_requiredSilent SSO check / token refresh in SPAs
loginForce re-authentication even if a session existsStep-up, sensitive actions
consentForce the consent screen againNew scopes

5.2 Why there are still three layers even with reuse

Reusing the Auth0 session (Layer 2) does not touch the corporate IdP session (Layer 3). If Auth0's session has expired but Okta's has not, Auth0 redirects to Okta, Okta sees its own cookie, and returns an assertion without prompting — federated SSO. So a "silent" enterprise login can still involve a round-trip to Okta. Each layer expires independently; Auth0's SSO inactivity and absolute session lifetimes are configured per tenant, while the IdP's lifetime is the customer's business.

5.3 Why SLO is hard, mechanically

Logout means deleting cookies, but a server can only delete cookies on its own domain. Your backend can clear app_session. To clear the Auth0 session you must redirect the browser to Auth0's /v2/logout endpoint (so Auth0 can clear its own cookie). To clear the IdP session you need the IdP's logout endpoint — and federated SLO must be explicitly enabled per connection, supported by the IdP, and is frequently flaky because IdPs implement SAML SLO and OIDC RP-Initiated/Back-Channel logout inconsistently. There is no single API call that synchronously kills all three.

Note

Auth0's logout is redirect-based by design: only the browser can carry the user through each domain so each authority can clear its own cookie. This is why SLO is a chain of redirects, not a function call.

6. Code Walkthrough — Org-Aware Login & Logout

6.1 Node.js (Express + express-openid-connect): organization login

// app.js — ProjectPilot backend
const express = require('express');
const { auth } = require('express-openid-connect');

const app = express();

app.use(auth({
  authRequired: false,
  auth0Logout: true,                       // route logout through Auth0 /v2/logout
  baseURL: 'https://app.projectpilot.com',
  clientID: process.env.AUTH0_CLIENT_ID,
  issuerBaseURL: `https://${process.env.AUTH0_DOMAIN}`,
  secret: process.env.SESSION_SECRET,
  authorizationParams: {
    response_type: 'code',
    scope: 'openid profile email',
  },
}));

// Start login for a SPECIFIC organization (B2B).
// organization=org_xxx tells Auth0 which org context to use.
app.get('/login/:orgId', (req, res) => {
  res.oidc.login({
    returnTo: '/dashboard',
    authorizationParams: { organization: req.params.orgId },
  });
});

// Let the user pick their org first. NOTE: Auth0's org picker is NOT triggered
// by prompt=select_account. Instead set the application's "Login Flow" to
// "Prompt for Organization" (client setting organization_require_behavior:
// "pre_login_prompt") in the Dashboard / Management API. Then simply omit the
// organization parameter and Auth0 renders the organization selection screen.
app.get('/login', (req, res) => {
  res.oidc.login({ returnTo: '/dashboard' });   // no organization → Auth0 shows the org picker
});

app.get('/dashboard', (req, res) => {
  const claims = req.oidc.user;            // includes org_id when org login was used
  res.send(`Hello ${claims.name} — org: ${claims.org_id}`);
});

app.listen(3000);

The decisive line is authorizationParams: { organization: 'org_xxx' }. It scopes the login to one tenant; the resulting ID token carries an org_id (and, if configured, org_name) claim, which your app must read to enforce tenant isolation.

6.2 Logout — clearing the right layers

// With auth0Logout:true, GET /logout clears the app cookie
// THEN redirects to https://TENANT/v2/logout?client_id=...&returnTo=...
// which clears the Auth0 (Layer 2) session cookie too.
//
// To also clear the IdP (Layer 3), federated logout must be enabled
// on the connection AND requested explicitly:
app.get('/logout-everywhere', (req, res) => {
  res.oidc.logout({
    returnTo: 'https://app.projectpilot.com/goodbye',
    logoutParams: { federated: '' },       // attempt upstream IdP logout
  });
});

6.3 React SPA (auth0-react): organization & silent SSO

import { Auth0Provider } from '@auth0/auth0-react';

<Auth0Provider

  }}
  useRefreshTokens={true}
  cacheLocation="localstorage"
>
  <App />
</Auth0Provider>

// LoginButton.jsx — silent SSO check happens automatically via
// checkSession() / getAccessTokenSilently() which call /authorize?prompt=none
import { useAuth0 } from '@auth0/auth0-react';
export function LoginButton() {
  const { loginWithRedirect, logout, isAuthenticated, user } = useAuth0();
  if (isAuthenticated)
    return <button onClick={() => logout({
      logoutParams: { returnTo: window.location.origin, federated: true }
    })}>Log out</button>;
  return <button onClick={() => loginWithRedirect()}>Log in</button>;
}

6.4 Spring Boot (Java) — reading the org claim & enforcing isolation

// SecurityConfig.java — resource server validating Auth0 access tokens
@Configuration
@EnableWebSecurity
public class SecurityConfig {
  @Bean
  SecurityFilterChain filterChain(HttpSecurity http) throws Exception {
    http.authorizeHttpRequests(a -> a.anyRequest().authenticated())
        .oauth2ResourceServer(o -> o.jwt(Customizer.withDefaults()));
    return http.build();
  }
}

// ProjectController.java — every query MUST be scoped by org_id
@RestController
class ProjectController {
  @GetMapping("/projects")
  List<Project> list(@AuthenticationPrincipal Jwt jwt) {
    String orgId = jwt.getClaimAsString("org_id");      // tenant boundary
    if (orgId == null) throw new AccessDeniedException("no org context");
    return repo.findByOrgId(orgId);                     // NEVER findAll()
  }
}

7. Practical Implementation — Organizations, Connections, HRD

7.1 Create an organization and assign connections (Management API)

# 1) Get a Management API token (client-credentials, mgmt audience)
TOKEN=$(curl -s --request POST "https://$AUTH0_DOMAIN/oauth/token" \
  --header 'content-type: application/json' \
  --data '{"client_id":"'$M2M_ID'","client_secret":"'$M2M_SECRET'",
           "audience":"https://'$AUTH0_DOMAIN'/api/v2/","grant_type":"client_credentials"}' \
  | jq -r .access_token)

# 2) Create the Acme organization
curl -s --request POST "https://$AUTH0_DOMAIN/api/v2/organizations" \
  --header "authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" --header 'content-type: application/json' \
  --data '{
    "name": "acme",
    "display_name": "Acme Corp",
    "branding": { "logo_url": "https://cdn.acme.com/logo.png",
                  "colors": { "primary": "#0055ff", "page_background": "#ffffff" } },
    "metadata": { "plan": "enterprise" }
  }'

# 3) Enable a connection (Acme's Okta SAML/OIDC) FOR this org only,
#    and turn on auto-membership so users from this connection auto-join.
curl -s --request POST \
  "https://$AUTH0_DOMAIN/api/v2/organizations/org_acme_123/enabled_connections" \
  --header "authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" --header 'content-type: application/json' \
  --data '{ "connection_id": "con_acmeOkta", "assign_membership_on_login": true }'

7.2 Home-Realm Discovery (HRD)

HRD answers "given who is logging in, which connection should I send them to?" so the user never has to choose. Auth0 supports several strategies:

  • Email-domain mapping — set domain_aliases on the enterprise connection (e.g. acme.com). Auth0's Identifier-First login asks for email, matches the domain, and routes to Acme Okta automatically.
  • Organization prompt — when organization is not pre-selected, Auth0 can show an org picker, then constrain connections to that org.
  • Per-org login URL — your app passes organization=org_xxx directly (e.g. via subdomain routing) and skips picking entirely.
# Add a domain alias so anyone @acme.com is auto-routed to Acme's Okta:
curl -s --request PATCH "https://$AUTH0_DOMAIN/api/v2/connections/con_acmeOkta" \
  --header "authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" --header 'content-type: application/json' \
  --data '{ "options": { "domain_aliases": ["acme.com"] } }'

7.3 Inviting and onboarding members

# Invite a new Acme member by email. Auth0 emails an invitation link;
# accepting it creates the user (if needed) and adds org membership.
curl -s --request POST \
  "https://$AUTH0_DOMAIN/api/v2/organizations/org_acme_123/invitations" \
  --header "authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" --header 'content-type: application/json' \
  --data '{
    "inviter":  { "name": "Acme Admin" },
    "invitee":  { "email": "newhire@acme.com" },
    "client_id": "'$CLIENT_ID'",
    "connection_id": "con_acmeOkta",
    "roles": ["rol_member"],
    "ttl_sec": 604800,
    "send_invitation_email": true
  }'

The invitation link carries invitation, organization, and organization_name query parameters. Your /login route must forward these to /authorize so the accept flow lands the user in the right org with the right roles.

// Accept-invitation route (Node): forward invitation params to Auth0
app.get('/accept', (req, res) => {
  const { invitation, organization } = req.query;
  res.oidc.login({
    returnTo: '/dashboard',
    authorizationParams: { invitation, organization },
  });
});

Production guidance:

  • One Auth0 tenant, many Organizations — do not spin up an Auth0 tenant per customer; that doesn't scale and Organizations exist precisely to avoid it.
  • Routing → org resolution at the edge. Map subdomain (or email domain via HRD) to org_id before /authorize.
  • Tenant isolation in two places — enforce org_id at the API (from the token) and store tenant_id on every row (defense in depth). Never rely on the token alone.
  • Actions enrich tokens with org_id, org roles, and app_metadata so downstream services authorize without extra lookups.

The number-one B2B vulnerability is the missing tenant filter. An access token proves who the user is and which org, but your code must still scope every read/write to that org. Treat org_id as a mandatory query predicate, not an optional one.

10. Performance Considerations

  • Silent SSO costs a redirect. prompt=none via a hidden iframe (or refresh tokens) avoids a full login UI but still hits /authorize. In SPAs prefer refresh tokens with rotation over iframe silent auth — third-party cookie blocking (Safari ITP, Chrome) breaks iframe-based silent SSO.
  • Federated round-trips. A cold enterprise login may bounce App → Auth0 → Okta → Auth0 → App. Cache nothing security-sensitive, but keep Auth0 session lifetimes long enough that Layer-2 reuse spares the Okta hop on subsequent apps.
  • Token size. Stuffing many org roles/permissions into the ID/access token inflates every request header. Put coarse claims in the token; fetch fine-grained permissions server-side or use a permissions endpoint.
  • Management API rate limits. Bulk inviting members or listing org membership hits API rate limits — batch, back off, and cache org metadata rather than calling per request.
  • JWKS caching. Resource servers should cache the tenant's JWKS keys (respecting rotation) instead of fetching per token validation.

11. Common Mistakes

  1. One Auth0 tenant per customer. Unmanageable; Organizations is the answer.
  2. Forgetting organization on /authorize. Users log in with no org context, org_id is absent, and tenant isolation silently fails.
  3. Enabling enterprise connections globally. Acme's Okta becomes usable across all orgs. Enable per organization.
  4. Logout that only clears the app cookie. The Auth0 SSO session survives, so the next login is silent and the user appears "not logged out."
  5. Assuming federated SLO just works. It must be enabled, supported by the IdP, and tested; many SAML IdPs implement SLO poorly.
  6. Trusting the token for authorization without re-checking org on the resource. Always assert the token's org_id matches the resource's tenant.
  7. Hardcoding one connection. Multi-tenant apps must select the connection dynamically (HRD / org).
  8. Long-lived, never-rotated sessions. Offboarded employees keep access. Use short inactivity lifetimes + back-channel logout.
  9. Putting all permissions in the token. Header bloat and stale data; fetch dynamic authz server-side.
  10. Mixing B2C and B2B users in one undifferentiated pool. Use organizations to separate company users from self-serve users.

12. Best Practices

Tip

Design the identity model before the data model. Decide what a "tenant" is (organization), how users join (invite vs. JIT via connection), and how org_id flows from login to token to query — then build tables around it.

  • One tenant, Organizations for B2B; keep self-serve users in their own org or a default connection.
  • Connections per organization, with assign_membership_on_login for JIT membership and domain_aliases for HRD.
  • Always pass organization and validate the org_id claim end-to-end.
  • Enrich tokens with an Action (org roles, app_metadata) and keep tokens lean.
  • Logout strategy by tier: app cookie → Auth0 /v2/logout → federated logout where required by contract; document and test all three.
  • Short SSO inactivity + absolute lifetimes; support IdP-initiated and back-channel logout for fast offboarding.
  • Delegated admin via org roles so customer admins invite/remove without you.
  • Defense in depth: token org_id + row-level tenant_id + isolation tests in CI.

13. Interview Questions (with Model Answers)

Q1. Explain SSO at the session level.

A. SSO works because multiple applications delegate authentication to a central authority (Auth0) that holds an authoritative session in its own cookie. The first app login establishes that Auth0 session; subsequent apps redirect to /authorize, Auth0 finds its session cookie and issues fresh tokens without prompting. So it's "many apps, one shared upstream session," controlled by the prompt parameter.

Q2. Why is Single Logout hard?

A. SSO created multiple independent sessions in different domains (app, Auth0, corporate IdP). A server can only delete cookies on its own domain, and there's no synchronous channel to kill all of them. Logout must be a chain of browser redirects so each authority clears its own cookie, and federated SLO depends on the IdP supporting and correctly implementing SAML/OIDC logout — which is inconsistent.

Q3. A customer demands their employees log in with corporate Okta and never have a password in our app. How?

A. Create an Organization for the customer, configure an enterprise SAML/OIDC connection to their Okta, enable that connection only for their org, and disable database/password connections for it. Login passes organization=org_xxx; HRD via domain_aliases routes @customer.com users to Okta. We store no password.

Q4. How do you prevent cross-tenant data leakage?

A. The login carries an org_id claim in the token; the API validates it and scopes every query by it, and we also store tenant_id per row as defense in depth. We add automated tests asserting that a token for org A can never read org B's data.

Q5. Difference between Organizations and just using app_metadata.company?

A. Organizations are first-class: per-org connections, branding, membership, roles, invitations, and an org_id claim — managed by Auth0 and customer admins. Stuffing a company field in metadata gives you none of the connection isolation, delegated admin, invitation flow, or HRD; you'd reinvent all of it poorly.

Q6. How does a single human belong to two companies?

A. One user identity can be a member of multiple organizations. Login is org-scoped, so the same user authenticates into org_acme or org_globex, and the token's org_id tells the app which context applies. The app can offer an org switcher that re-runs login with a different organization.

Q7. What's the role of prompt=none?

A. It performs a silent authentication: if an Auth0 session exists, tokens are returned without UI; if not, it errors with login_required. SPAs use it (or refresh tokens) to silently establish/refresh sessions, though third-party cookie restrictions make refresh-token rotation the more robust choice.

14. Certification Tips

  • Know the exact claim names: org_id and org_name appear in tokens for org logins. The organization parameter is on /authorize.
  • Connections are enabled per organization; remember assign_membership_on_login (auto-membership) and domain_aliases (HRD).
  • Logout endpoint is /v2/logout; federated triggers upstream IdP logout; returnTo must be allowlisted.
  • Two B2B login models: prompt for organization (set the app's Login Flow / organization_require_behavior to pre_login_prompt and omit the parameter — Auth0 shows the picker) vs. organization pre-selected (your app passes organization=org_xxx, behavior no_prompt). Know when each is used — prompt=select_account does not trigger the org picker.
  • Invitations are created via the Management API under /organizations/{id}/invitations; they're email-bound, one-time, and time-limited.
  • Session layers: be able to name app vs. Auth0 SSO vs. IdP session and which one each lifetime setting controls.
  • One tenant, many organizations is the recommended B2B model — a frequent exam point.

19. Cheat Sheet

SESSION LAYERS      app cookie | Auth0 SSO cookie | IdP cookie  (3, independent)
LOGIN (org)         /authorize?...&organization=org_xxx&[invitation=...]
SILENT SSO          /authorize?...&prompt=none   → ok | error=login_required
FORCE LOGIN         /authorize?...&prompt=login
TOKEN CLAIMS        org_id, org_name, roles[] (via Action)
LOGOUT (app+Auth0)  GET /v2/logout?client_id=...&returnTo=<allowlisted>
LOGOUT (federated)  add &federated  → also signs out upstream IdP
HRD                 connection.options.domain_aliases = ["acme.com"]
JIT MEMBERSHIP      enabled_connections.assign_membership_on_login = true
ENABLE CONN PER ORG POST /api/v2/organizations/{id}/enabled_connections
INVITE MEMBER       POST /api/v2/organizations/{id}/invitations (email-bound, 1x, ttl)
ISOLATION RULE      token.org_id  AND  row.tenant_id   (defense in depth)
B2B MODEL           ONE Auth0 tenant + MANY Organizations  (never tenant-per-customer)

20. Key Takeaways

Key

Three session layers explain everything. SSO is reuse of the shared Auth0 (and IdP) session; SLO is hard because you must reach back across all three domains, each of which only its owner can clear.

Key

Organizations are the B2B primitive. One Auth0 tenant, many Organizations; per-org connections, branding, roles, invitations, and an org_id claim give you real tenant isolation and delegated admin out of the box.

Key

Always carry org context. Pass organization on login, validate org_id in the API, and store tenant_id per row. The missing tenant filter is the #1 B2B vulnerability.

Key

HRD removes friction via domain_aliases and per-org login URLs, and invitations (email-bound, one-time, time-limited) onboard members safely with delegated admin.

Key

Logout is a tiered redirect chain: app cookie → Auth0 /v2/logout → federated IdP logout. Design and test it explicitly, and use short session lifetimes for fast offboarding.
